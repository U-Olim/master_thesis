\section{Literature Review}

Quantile regression, introduced by \textcite{koenker_bassett_1978}, describes how covariates are related to different parts of a conditional outcome distribution rather than only to its mean. This is useful when treatment effects differ across the outcome distribution. Standard quantile regression, however, does not generally recover structural treatment effects when treatment is endogenous, because treatment may be related to unobserved factors that also affect the outcome. \textcite{chernozhukov_hansen_2005} address this problem through instrumental variable quantile regression (IVQR). Their framework combines excluded instruments with restrictions on structural ranks to identify quantile-specific treatment effects in the presence of endogeneity.

A central feature of IVQR is the distinction between instrument validity and identification. Under the structural assumptions of \textcite{chernozhukov_hansen_2005}, instrument exogeneity and rank restrictions imply conditional moment conditions that can be used for estimation. Rank invariance assumes that an individual's structural rank remains the same across treatment states, while the weaker rank-similarity condition allows the rank to change as long as its conditional distribution is preserved. These assumptions connect observed outcomes to structural quantiles. They do not guarantee that the structural effect is precisely identified. The instrument must also provide enough treatment variation to distinguish the true structural effect from competing candidate values. Because IVQR moment conditions are quantile specific and nonlinear, conventional first-stage measures provide useful information about instrument relevance but do not fully describe identification at a particular quantile \parencite{chernozhukov_hansen_2005,chernozhukov_hansen_wuthrich_2017}.

\textcite{chernozhukov_hansen_2006} develop practical estimation and inference methods for the IVQR model under regular identification. Their inverse quantile regression approach considers candidate values of the endogenous-treatment coefficient and uses conventional quantile regression to estimate the remaining parameters. This reduces the difficult optimization problem to the lower-dimensional treatment parameter and provides a practical route for IVQR estimation. \textcite{chernozhukov_hansen_2008} extend this approach to inference that is robust to weak identification. Instead of relying on a normal approximation centered on a precisely estimated treatment effect, their procedure tests each candidate treatment-effect value directly and constructs a confidence region by retaining the values that are not rejected. The resulting region can be wide, asymmetric, or disconnected when the instruments contain little identifying information. This is an important distinction for our thesis: weak-identification-robust inference may remain valid while providing little information about the value of the structural effect. \textcite{chernozhukov_hansen_jansson_2009} develop a different finite-sample conditional inference procedure for quantile regression models.

The IVQR weak-identification problem is related to the broader literature on weak instrumental variables. \textcite{anderson_rubin_1949} provide an early approach based on testing structural parameter values without requiring a precisely estimated first stage. \textcite{staiger_stock_1997} show why conventional instrumental-variable approximations can perform poorly when instruments are weak, while \textcite{stock_wright_2000} develop weak-identification-robust methods for generalized method-of-moments (GMM) models. \textcite{kleibergen_2002} and \textcite{moreira_2003} provide further robust testing procedures, and \textcite{andrews_stock_sun_2019} review the modern weak-instrument literature and its implications for empirical research. These contributions establish the importance of inference procedures that remain reliable when identification is weak. However, the usual linear-IV diagnostics do not provide a direct measure of quantile-specific IVQR identification. For this reason, we report the first-stage statistics to describe the ordering of instrument relevance across the Monte Carlo designs rather than formal IVQR weak-instrument thresholds.

A separate literature addresses inference with many controls or other high-dimensional nuisance parameters. \textcite{belloni_chernozhukov_2011} study \(\ell_1\)-penalized quantile regression under approximate sparsity and develop a pivotal method for choosing the penalty level. This type of regularization makes estimation feasible when the number of possible controls is large, although regularization can itself introduce estimation error. \textcite{belloni_chernozhukov_kato_2019} show how orthogonal score functions can support valid inference on a parameter of interest in high-dimensional approximately sparse quantile regression models. More generally, \textcite{chernozhukov_et_al_2018} develop the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework. Its central idea is to construct Neyman-orthogonal scores whose population moments are less sensitive, to first order, to small errors in estimated nuisance parameters. Additionally, Cross-fitting can reduce overfitting dependence by estimating nuisance functions and evaluating the score on different parts of the sample. Orthogonality and cross-fitting should therefore be distinguished: orthogonality is a property of the score, while cross-fitting is a way of organizing estimation across observations.

These high-dimensional methods address nuisance estimation rather than weak identification. In particular, regularization and Neyman orthogonality cannot make an instrument more relevant or create information that is absent from the data. \textcite{chen_huang_tien_2021} combine IVQR with high-dimensional nuisance estimation by developing a DML-IVQR procedure based on a Neyman-orthogonal score. For each candidate treatment-effect value, nuisance parameters are estimated with regularization and the resulting score is used for GMM estimation and candidate-specific inference. Their high-dimensional Monte Carlo design compares Oracle-GMM, which knows the relevant controls, Full-GMM, which uses all controls without regularization, and DML-IVQR, which uses regularized nuisance estimation. They also construct weak-identification-robust confidence regions for these estimators. Their results show that DML-IVQR can substantially improve on an unregularized high-dimensional specification and can approach the oracle benchmark in their baseline designs.

The implementation of \textcite{chen_huang_tien_2021} is especially important for our thesis because it provides the baseline Monte Carlo environment that is preserved and extended. Their main high-dimensional simulation considers a fixed level of instrument relevance. It therefore does not systematically examine how the same estimators and candidate-specific inference procedures behave as instrument relevance becomes progressively weaker. This distinction matters because successful high-dimensional nuisance estimation does not imply that the structural parameter remains well identified. A DML procedure may estimate nuisance components accurately while the instruments still provide too little information to distinguish competing structural effects.

Other work illustrates continuing developments in IVQR estimation and high-dimensional quantile methods. \textcite{wuthrich_2019} develops an approach for estimating quantile treatment effects with endogenous binary treatment and instruments, providing an alternative to directly optimizing the standard IVQR objective. More recently, \textcite{jin_sun_2025} develop a Neyman-orthogonal moment for a nonparametric IVQR model with high-dimensional covariates and focus on unconditional quantile treatment effects. They recommend cross-fitting and automatic DML methods for their setting. These approaches differ from the linear structural IVQR model and preserved Monte Carlo implementation studied in our thesis.

The literature therefore leaves a specific finite-sample question that is central to our thesis. Existing works provide structural IVQR model, weak-identification-robust inference, high-dimensional regularization, and orthogonal-score methods, but there is a limited evidence on how weak instrument relevance affects these components jointly in the preserved high-dimensional DML-IVQR design of \textcite{chen_huang_tien_2021}. In our thesis we addresse that question by systematically reducing instrument relevance while keeping the remaining features of the Monte Carlo environment as stable as possible. It evaluates how point-estimation accuracy, coverage, power, and accepted-set informativeness change across instrument-relevance settings, quantiles, and estimators. The contribution is therefore a controlled finite-sample extension of the existing DML-IVQR design of \textcite{chen_huang_tien_2021}.
