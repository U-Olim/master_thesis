\section{Results}
The analysis proceeds in four steps: verifying the relevance ordering induced by $\kappa$ and examining point-estimation accuracy; assessing finite-sample coverage; evaluating accepted-set informativeness and power; and comparing quantiles and estimators. The central question is how quickly deterioration appears, and through which dimensions, in this high-dimensional IVQR design. Reading these measures together distinguishes inaccurate estimation, poorly calibrated inference, and tests that retain too many competing effects to be informative.
\suppressfloats[t]

\subsection{Instrument Relevance and Point Estimation}

Table~\ref{tab:strength-calibration} establishes that the four values of $\kappa$ produce clearly ordered excluded-instrument relevance settings, using the first-stage $F$-statistic and partial $R^2$ as descriptive diagnostics.

\input{tables/table_strength_calibration}

Both diagnostics fall strongly as $\kappa$ decreases. For $n=500$, the median $F$-statistic falls from $101.17$ at $\kappa=1$ to $18.71$ at $\kappa=0.50$, $4.74$ at $\kappa=0.25$, and $1.22$ at $\kappa=0.10$; the corresponding endpoints for $n=1000$ are $212.82$ and $2.04$. Partial $R^2$ is comparatively stable across sample sizes, whereas $F$ increases with $n$. The design therefore varies the instruments' explanatory contribution while sample size affects the amount of information available. These diagnostics describe relative relevance within the experiment; they are not quantile-specific weak-IV thresholds.

The two diagnostics separate signal strength from sample information. Partial $R^2$ falls monotonically from approximately $0.29$ and $0.30$ at strong relevance to $0.005$ and $0.004$ at the weakest setting for the smaller and larger samples, respectively. Its relative stability across sample sizes reflects a similar underlying explanatory share, while its decline across $\kappa$ indicates that the designed population signal is changing. The $F$-statistic rises with sample size because the same explanatory share is supported by more observations and estimated more precisely. The clean ordering at both sample sizes confirms that the intervention works as designed, without supplying formal IVQR identification thresholds.

Table~\ref{tab:main-performance} is the central finite-sample performance table. It reports Bias, MAE, RMSE, Coverage, and the median accepted-set measure for $n=1000$, $\tau\in\{0.10,0.50,0.90\}$, all four $\kappa$ values, and Oracle-GMM, Full-GMM, and DML-IVQR-BC.

\input{tables/table_main_performance}

As instrument relevance declines, MAE and RMSE generally increase for all three estimators and across the reported quantiles. At $\tau=0.50$, moving from $\kappa=1$ to $\kappa=0.10$ raises RMSE from $0.072$ to $0.945$ for Oracle, from $0.100$ to $1.018$ for Full, and from $0.104$ to $0.991$ for DML. Oracle deterioration shows that the phenomenon is not generated solely by high-dimensional nuisance estimation: knowing the relevant controls does not replace instrument information. Under the frozen convention $\mathrm{Bias}=\alpha_0-\widehat{\alpha}$, positive Bias means that estimates are below the truth on average.

The progression is visible before the weakest setting. At $\kappa=1$, estimates are relatively concentrated, particularly at the median. At $\kappa=0.50$, deterioration is already apparent, although useful signal remains. By $\kappa=0.25$, MAE and RMSE have increased markedly; at $\kappa=0.10$, errors are large across estimators. Modest Bias alongside much larger MAE and RMSE means that positive and negative replication errors partly cancel. Weak relevance therefore manifests importantly as dispersion rather than necessarily a common directional displacement.

Oracle-GMM knows the designated relevant control set and removes the high-dimensional variable-selection problem by construction. Its deterioration provides a benchmark for separating the common relevance problem from additional high-dimensional estimation difficulties. The shared loss of accuracy cannot be attributed solely to Lasso, nuisance selection, or DML regularization: all three procedures face reduced information from the excluded instruments.

\subsection{Coverage and Finite-Sample Calibration}

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-main} and the Coverage column of Table~\ref{tab:main-performance} show that empirical coverage is not uniformly close to the nominal level of $0.95$. Coverage is evaluated directly at the true structural quantile effect, independently of the numerical grid. Weak-identification robustness of the asymptotic procedure does not imply exact finite-sample coverage at the simulated sample sizes.

\input{tables/figure_coverage_main}

Because each reported coverage rate is based on $R=500$ Monte Carlo replications, the simulation itself introduces sampling uncertainty. For an estimated rejection or coverage probability $\widehat p$, the Monte Carlo standard error is
\[
\operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat p)
=
\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)}{R}}.
\]
With $R=500$, the largest possible MCSE is approximately $0.022$, attained near $\widehat p=0.5$; at the nominal coverage probability $0.95$, the corresponding MCSE is approximately $0.010$. Consequently, very small differences between nearby coverage rates should not be given substantive weight. The large deviations emphasized here are materially larger than Monte Carlo uncertainty: coverage values around $0.50$, $0.63$, or $0.86$, for example, remain far from the nominal $0.95$ benchmark relative to their simulation standard errors.

Differences of one or two percentage points warrant caution, whereas deviations of tens of percentage points from nominal coverage are far too large to be explained by the sampling uncertainty associated with $R=500$ replications.

Distortions occur even at $\kappa=1$ and vary substantially by estimator and quantile. For $n=1000$, Full coverage is $0.502$ at $\tau=0.10$ and $0.530$ at $\tau=0.90$. DML coverage is $0.632$ at $\tau=0.75$ and $0.498$ at $\tau=0.90$, but $0.968$ at $\tau=0.10$. Oracle coverage is $0.860$ at both $\tau=0.10$ and $\tau=0.75$, showing that high-dimensional nuisance estimation is not the sole explanation for undercoverage.

Coverage should be close to $0.95$ when the asymptotic chi-square distribution approximates the finite-sample null distribution well. The strong-relevance differences instead reveal calibration heterogeneity across estimator--quantile combinations. Increasing the sample size does not uniformly remove these distortions: the larger sample still contains substantial undercoverage. Small differences between the two sample sizes are less informative than the persistence of large deviations.

Calibration and weak identification are distinct issues. The robust test construction avoids requiring a strongly identified point estimator in the usual way, but still uses an asymptotic chi-square cutoff. Weak-identification robustness concerns the limiting inference; calibration concerns its approximation at the simulated sample sizes. Undercoverage already present with strong relevance therefore cannot be attributed to weak identification alone.

As relevance becomes very weak, the test may reject few candidates, retaining the truth frequently while accepted sets cover most of the primary domain. Such high coverage measures calibration and can coexist with very low informativeness and power. An increase in coverage as relevance weakens is therefore not automatically an improvement: truth retention may rise because the procedure rejects fewer values generally. Coverage must be read alongside the evidence on exclusion of false candidates.

\subsection{Confidence-Set Informativeness and Power}

Figure~\ref{fig:accepted-measure-main} shows the median grid-based accepted-set measure within $A_0=[-1,3]$. Panels (a) and (b) report $n=500$ and $n=1000$, respectively, allowing comparison across sample sizes for the same quantiles and estimators. The plotted quantity measures acceptance inside the four-unit primary domain, not unrestricted confidence-region length.

\input{tables/figure_accepted_measure_main}

The measure rises strongly as $\kappa$ falls, and at $\kappa=0.10$ accepted sets often occupy nearly the whole primary domain. For $n=1000$ and $\tau=0.50$, the Oracle, Full, and DML medians rise respectively from $0.200$, $0.400$, and $0.300$ at $\kappa=1$ to $3.900$, $3.800$, and $3.900$ at $\kappa=0.10$. Increasing $n$ generally improves localization, but does not solve the weakest-relevance case. The accepted-set measure is deliberately defined on the fixed primary domain $A_0=[-1,3]$; the separate domain diagnostic is reserved for the Appendix.

Small accepted-set measures under strong relevance indicate that many competing candidates are excluded. Expansion at intermediate relevance reflects a progressively less localized effect. A value near four means that almost the entire primary domain survives testing, not that the unrestricted confidence region has been shown to have length four. It is direct evidence of low localization within $A_0$.

The sample-size comparison is most favorable where useful instrument signal remains: additional observations generally narrow the accepted set. At the weakest relevance, however, measures remain large even in the larger sample. More observations help, but do not fully compensate for the information deficit at these simulated sizes; two sample sizes do not establish an asymptotic conclusion.

Figure~\ref{fig:power-main} reports rejection probabilities under moderate false alternatives for $n=1000$: panel (a) uses $\Delta=-0.50$ and panel (b) uses $\Delta=+0.50$. Rejection probabilities fall strongly as $\kappa$ decreases, so false candidate treatment effects survive more frequently.

\input{tables/figure_power_main}

The median-quantile results show a substantial loss of discrimination even at intermediate relevance. At $\tau=0.50$ and $\Delta=-0.50$, power falls from $1.000$ through $0.850$ and $0.316$ to $0.082$ for Oracle, and from $1.000$ through $0.802$ and $0.312$ to $0.104$ for DML across the four declining relevance settings. Full power falls from $0.998$ to $0.076$ between the strongest and weakest settings. At the positive alternative, the corresponding weakest-relevance probabilities are $0.092$, $0.098$, and $0.094$. Power is the fraction of replications rejecting the specified false effect $\alpha_0(\tau)+\Delta$; its decline directly records a reduced ability to distinguish that effect from the truth. Positive and negative displacements need not have equal power because IVQR moments are nonlinear in the candidate parameter. The finite-sample criterion need not be symmetric around the truth, so equal displacements can produce different rejection probabilities. This asymmetry alone is not evidence of point-estimation bias.

Figures~\ref{fig:accepted-measure-main} and~\ref{fig:power-main} reveal the same loss of information from complementary perspectives: accepted sets ask which candidates survive, while power asks whether specific false candidates are rejected. As relevance falls, survival expands and rejection declines. Their agreement is the clearest evidence here that weak relevance reduces inferential informativeness even with a test construction designed for weak-identification robustness.

\subsection{Quantile Heterogeneity and Estimator Comparison}

Taken together, Table~\ref{tab:main-performance} and Figures~\ref{fig:coverage-main}--\ref{fig:power-main} show that weakening relevance worsens performance across quantiles, although the magnitude differs. Tail quantiles often display larger estimation errors or more severe calibration problems, but there is no universal ordering between the center and tails or between the two tails.

IVQR identification is quantile specific because the density of the structural residual near zero enters the score and information structure. Tail thresholds commonly have fewer observations effectively nearby, while nuisance estimation and residualization also vary across quantiles. The same relevance setting can therefore yield different accuracy, coverage, and power. Tail deterioration is common, but the five quantiles have no universal monotone performance ranking.

Oracle generally provides the strongest benchmark, yet deteriorates sharply as relevance weakens. Full estimates all high-dimensional controls without regularization and often has larger error or lower power. DML often improves on Full, but does not dominate it in every cell or reproduce Oracle uniformly. Its substantial coverage distortions in some quantiles also show that gains in accuracy need not imply gains in calibration. These differences cannot be attributed solely to regularization because the estimators also use different residualization conventions.

The experiment answers the research question along four dimensions. Weaker relevance substantially worsens point-estimation accuracy; accepted candidates expand and power falls, revealing reduced informativeness. These effects also occur for Oracle, so they are not solely high-dimensional nuisance-estimation phenomena. Coverage adds a separate calibration dimension that varies across estimator and quantile and does not move mechanically with informativeness. DML can reduce some high-dimensional nuisance-estimation costs, but cannot restore information that weak instruments do not supply.
